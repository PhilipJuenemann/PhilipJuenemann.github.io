1.4 Implementing Iterative Denoising

In part 1.3, you should see that the denoising UNet does a much better job of projecting the image onto the natural image manifold, but it does get worse as you add more noise. This makes sense, as the problem is much harder with more noise!

But diffusion models are designed to denoise iteratively. In this part we implement this iterative process. We skip steps to speed things up, using a stride of 30 timesteps (starting at 990 and ending at 0). This creates a strided_timesteps list.

The iterative denoising process applies a specific update formula to move from a noisy image at timestep t to a less noisy image at timestep t'. This involves estimating the clean image x0 and interpolating.

1.6 Classifier Free Guidance (CFG)

In order to improve the quality of generated images, we use Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG). This technique computes both a conditional noise estimate (based on a text prompt) and an unconditional noise estimate (based on an empty prompt). The final noise estimate is a linear combination of these two:

ε = εu + γ(εc - εu)

where γ controls the guidance strength. Setting γ > 1 pushes the generation towards the conditional prompt, resulting in higher quality images.

1.7 Image-to-image Translation

In this part, we apply the SDEdit algorithm. By taking an original image, adding varying levels of noise, and then denoising it using the diffusion model, we can generate variations of the original image. The more noise added (lower starting index i_start, higher timestep t), the more the model "hallucinates" and deviates from the original. As the noise level decreases, the result remains closer to the original.

1.7.2 Inpainting

Inpainting allows us to replace specific parts of an image with new content while preserving the rest. This is achieved by using a binary mask. During the diffusion denoising loop, we force the pixels outside the mask to match the original image (with appropriate noise added), while allowing the model to generate new content inside the mask.

1.8 Visual Anagrams

Visual Anagrams allow us to create optical illusions using diffusion models. By averaging the noise estimates from two different prompts—one applied to the original image and one applied to a flipped version—we can generate an image that looks like one thing right-side up and something else when flipped upside down.

The algorithm proceeds as follows:

ε1 = CFG(UNet(xt, t, p1))

ε2 = flip(CFG(UNet(flip(xt), t, p2)))

ε = (ε1 + ε2) / 2

1.9 Hybrid Images

In this final part, we implement Factorized Diffusion to create hybrid images. Similar to Visual Anagrams, we combine noise estimates from two different prompts. However, instead of spatial flipping, we combine the low frequencies of one noise estimate with the high frequencies of another. This creates an image that looks like one thing from afar (low frequency) and another when viewed up close (high frequency).

The algorithm is:

ε1 = CFG(UNet(xt, t, p1))

ε2 = CFG(UNet(xt, t, p2))

ε = flowpass(ε1) + fhighpass(ε2)

Part B: Training Diffusion Models

In this part, we train our own flow matching model on the MNIST dataset.

Part 1: Training a Single-Step Denoising UNet

We begin by training a simple one-step denoiser. Given a noisy image z, we train a denoiser Dθ to map z to a clean image x by optimizing an L2 loss.

1.1 Implementing the UNet

The denoiser is implemented as a UNet, consisting of downsampling and upsampling blocks with skip connections. The architecture includes standard operations like Conv2d, BatchNorm2d, GELU, ConvTranspose2d, and AvgPool2d. The model uses composed operations to increase depth without changing tensor dimensions, except where downsampling or upsampling is explicitly performed.

1.2 Using the UNet to Train a Denoiser

To train the denoiser, we generate training pairs (z, x) by adding noise to clean MNIST digits. The noising process is defined as z = x + σ * ε, where ε is random Gaussian noise.

Observed Patterns Analysis

The images generated from pure noise resemble blurry, averaged amalgams of various digits rather than distinct numbers. Instead of producing a clear '3' or '7', the output looks like a superposition of multiple digits from the training set.

This phenomenon occurs because the model minimizes Mean Squared Error (MSE) without any conditioning signal. In the absence of information guiding it toward a specific digit, the optimal strategy to minimize MSE is to predict the mean of the possible outcomes. This effectively results in the model outputting the "centroid" or average of the MNIST dataset's digit distribution—a blurry, ambiguous shape that minimizes the expected squared distance to all training examples.

Part 2: Training a Flow Matching Model

We just saw that one-step denoising does not work well for generative tasks. Instead, we need to iteratively denoise the image, and we will do so with flow matching. Here, we will iteratively denoise an image by training a UNet model to predict the "flow" from our noisy data to clean data. In our flow matching setup, we sample a pure noise image z and generate a realistic image x.

2.1 Adding Time Conditioning to UNet

We inject a scalar timestep t into our UNet to condition it. This allows the model to know "where" it is in the denoising process. We use Fully-Connected Blocks (FCBlocks) to modulate the features in the UNet based on the timestep.

Removing the Learning Rate Scheduler

When training without the exponential learning rate scheduler, I found that using a constant learning rate of 2e-3 worked best.

The scheduler originally began at 1e-2 to enable rapid early learning, then gradually reduced it (by a factor of ~0.794 each epoch) so the model could refine its weights and reach a stable state.

Keeping a fixed moderate rate (5e-3) throughout training proved problematic - the optimizer kept making steps that were too large in later stages, preventing convergence to a quality solution. This resulted in worse generated samples at epoch 10, even though the training loss appeared lower.

What Changed:

  • Eliminated the exponential learning rate scheduler
  • Applied a fixed learning rate of 2e-3 throughout training
  • Kept the same total training time (20 epochs)
